LIBER TERTIUS

Edidit Marcus Deufert

1 O tenebris tantis tam clarum extollere lumen

2 qui primus potuisti inlustrans commoda uitae,

3 te sequor, o Graiae gentis decus, inque tuis nunc

4 ficta pedum pono pressis uestigia signis,

5 non ita certandi cupidus quam propter amorem

6 quod te imitari aueo; quid enim contendat hirundo

7 cycnis, aut quidnam tremulis facere artubus haedi

8 consimile in cursu possint et fortis equi uis?

9 tu, pater, es rerum inuentor, tu patria nobis

10 suppeditas praecepta, tuisque ex, inclute, chartis,

11 floriferis ut apes in saltibus omnia libant,

12 omnia nos itidem depascimur aurea dicta,

13 aurea, perpetua semper dignissima uita.

14 nam simul ac ratio tua coepit uociferari

15 naturam rerum, diuina mente coorta,

16 diffugiunt animi terrores, moenia mundi

17 discedunt, totum uideo per inane geri res.

18 apparet diuum numen sedesque quietae,

19 quas neque concutiunt uenti nec nubila nimbis

20 aspergunt neque nix acri concreta pruina

21 cana cadens uiolat, semperque innubilus aether

22 integit et large diffuso lumine ridet.

23 Omnia suppeditat porro natura, neque ulla

24 res animi pacem delibat tempore in ullo.

25 at contra nusquam apparent Acherusia templa,

26 nec tellus obstat quin omnia dispiciantur,

27 sub pedibus quaecumque infra per inane geruntur.

28 his ibi me rebus quaedam diuina uoluptas

29 percipit atque horror, quod sic natura tua ui

30 tam manifesta patens ex omni parte retecta est.

31 Et quoniam docui cunctarum exordia rerum

32 qualia sint et quam uariis distantia formis

33 sponte sua uolitent aeterno percita motu ,

34 quoue modo possint res ex his quaeque creari,

35 hasce secundum res animi natura uidetur

36 atque animae claranda meis iam uersibus esse

37 et metus ille foras praeceps Acheruntis agendus,

38 funditus humanam qui uitam turbat ab imo

39 omnia suffundens mortis nigrore neque ullam

40 esse uoluptatem liquidam puramque relinquit.

41 nam quod saepe homines morbos magis esse timendos

42 infamemque ferunt uitam quam Tartara leti

43 et se scire animi naturam sanguinis esse

46 aut etiam uenti, si fert ita forte uoluntas,

44 nec prorsum quicquam nostrae rationis egere,

45 hinc licet aduertas animum magis omnia laudis

47 iactari causa quam quod res ipsa probetur.

48 extorres idem patria longeque fugati

49 conspectu ex hominum, foedati crimine turpi,

50 omnibus aerumnis adfecti denique, uiuunt,

51 et quocumque tamen miseri uenere parentant

52 et nigras mactant pecudes et manibus diuis

53 inferias mittunt multoque in rebus acerbis

54 acrius aduertunt animos ad religionem.

55 quo magis in dubiis hominem spectare periclis

56 conuenit aduersisque in rebus noscere qui sit;

57 nam uerae uoces tum demum pectore ab imo

58 eliciuntur, et eripitur persona †manare†.

59 denique auarities et honorum caeca cupido,

60 quae miseros homines cogunt transcendere fines

61 iuris et interdum socios scelerum atque ministros

62 noctes atque dies niti praestante labore

63 ad summas emergere opes , haec uulnera uitae

64 non minimam partem mortis formidine aluntur.

65 turpis enim ferme contemptus et acris egestas

66 semota ab dulci uita stabilique uidetur

67 et quasi iam leti portas cunctarier ante;

68 unde homines dum se falso terrore coacti

69 effugisse uolunt longe longeque remosse,

70 sanguine ciuili rem conflant diuitiasque

71 conduplicant auidi, caedem caede accumulantes,

72 crudeles gaudent in tristi funere fratris

73 et consanguineum mensas odere timentque.

74 Consimili ratione ab eodem saepe timore

75 macerat inuidia ante oculos illum esse potentem,

76 illum aspectari, claro qui incedit honore;

77 ipsi se in tenebris uolui caenoque queruntur.

78 intereunt partim statuarum et nominis ergo.

79 et saepe usque adeo, mortis formidine, uitae

80 percipit humanos odium lucisque uidendae,

81 ut sibi consciscant maerenti pectore letum,

82 obliti fontem curarum hunc esse timorem,

83 hunc uexare pudorem, hunc uincula amicitiai

84 rumpere et in summa pietatem euertere fundo.

85 nam iam saepe homines patriam carosque parentis

86 prodiderunt, uitare Acherusia templa petentes.

87 nam ueluti pueri trepidant atque omnia caecis

88 in tenebris metuunt, sic nos in luce timemus

89 interdum, nihilo quae sunt metuenda magis quam

90 quae pueri in tenebris pauitant finguntque futura.

91 hunc igitur terrorem animi tenebrasque necessest

92 non radii solis neque lucida tela diei

93 discutiant, sed naturae species ratioque.

Translation

By David R. Slavitt

1In the murk of our darkness, you, Epicurus, raised your blazing lantern

2to show us the blessings of life.

3And I follow you,

4walking with confident footsteps the trails that you have blazed,

5not to be your rival, but in admiration and love,

6and happy to have your example to guide me. How could I think of competing with you? A swallow might as well vie

7with swans, or a young kid on its wobbly legs presume to race

8alongside a strong horse.

9You are our father, our leader, our pioneer, discovering truths that you share with us.

10You are our guide, and from your writings,

11You are like one of those bees that goes out to find the flowers and then brings back word to the hive of where the sweet nectar is for them all.

12So do we feed on your words

13of perdurable gold that are worthy of everlasting life.

14We begin to follow your thoughts, your arguments, and your reasons,

15about the nature of things, sprung from a divine mind,

16and our mind’s terrors abate, while the opaque walls of the world

17open wide to exhibit the intricate actions and movements that go on all the time throughout the limitless void.

18Through you, I see the gods in their tranquil dwelling places,

19where no harsh winds ever blow, where no dark clouds approach with the threat of violent storms,

20and it never snows or freezes,

21but a gentle air surrounds them attendant on their pleasures,

22and smiles in an expanse of bounteous light.

23nature never obtrudes on their lofty peace of mind

24but amplifies their delight.

25To them, Acheron’s gloomy regions are an unlikely story.

26They look down on the earth beneath

27and watch with the same keen understanding as you, Epicurus, have taught to us,

28the intricateness of nature’s inner workings

29that I contemplate with awe and an all but divine delight

30in what you expound and explain.

31I have already described the atoms, the seeds of things,

32their different shapes

33and motions as they fly about on their own,

34and how all things in the world are derived from their combinations.

35The next subject to which we turn our attention is the mind, the animus,

36and the spirit, the anima,

37and the way to get rid of the fear of death that troubles us all,

38tainting all our delights

39and shadowing every moment with Acheron’s gloom.

40[leaving no joy pure and unalloyed.]

41More than they fear the pains of disease

42or guilt and shame, men dread the bottomless chasm of death.

43Some there are who claim that the soul, which is in the blood,

44or perhaps in the air we breathe, survives and never dies,

45and they have no use for logic and reason.

46But I shall explain

47their ideas are fantastic and their hopes not based upon fact.

48Take some poor fellow,

49banished for his disgraceful crime,

50living beyond the edge of nowhere, but still alive.

51What does he do but continue his sacrifices

52of cattle and prayers to his forebears’ ghosts,

53and, in his misery,

54hope for a less wretched life than this in another and better world?

55It is always in such adversity that we learn what a man is made of,

56what truth or what superstition informs his thoughts and actions,

57and what resources he brings with which to confront his troubles.

58The mask is torn away, and you look at his real face.

59Bizarrely enough it is that fear of death that ruins the life of a man

60as he strives for wealth and power, scheming and plotting,

61forcing miserable mortals to transgress all bounds of law

62day and night to climb the greasy pole by fair means or foul,

63striving to emerge into the heights of sovereign power.

64These wounds of life are nourished in no small part by the dread of death.

65Even though he’s afraid of men’s scorn and grinding poverty,

66his real worry is that he may wake up one day far removed from a sweet and stable life,

67lingering already, as it were, before the very gates of death;

68and from this, driven by baseless panic, he strives to escape,

69wishing to banish the specter far, far away from himself.

70The sweetness of life and its simple pleasures he overlooks,

71as he vies with other men, amassing wealth, greedy, adding slaughter to slaughter,

72ruthless even to doing murder, rejoicing in the death of a brother,

73and fearing and loathing the banquet tables of his own kinsmen.

74For like reason he never ventures to dine without terror,

75fearing his wine goblet may be poisoned as well.

76In the same way he is consumed with bitter envy

77of someone with pomp and power who parades through the town in glory,

78while he himself wallows in obscurity’s murky shadows.

79Men wear themselves out and perish for the sake of statues

80and an enduring name proclaimed on the marble plinth.

81The fear of death corrupts the love of life and the light,

82breeding self-hatred and driving men to suicide,

83forgetting that this very terror is the fountain of all their cares,

84the poison that shatters modesty and breaks the bonds of friendship,

85overturning piety from its deepest foundations.

86For their fear of death some men dishonor themselves and betray

87their friends, even their dear parents and fatherland,

88all in the desperate hope of postponing that journey to Acheron’s bleak kingdom.

89Just as children in blind darkness tremble and imagine terrible monsters,

90so do we, in the light of day, fear things that are no more frightful

91than what children dread in the night, imagining ghastly horrors of decease.

92This dread in the mind’s dark recess neither the rays of the sun

93nor daylight can dispel, but only reason and the contemplation of nature’s laws.

v. 7 tremulis facere artubus haedi (III.7) echoes a similar internal parallel at II.367 (tremulis cum uocibus haedi), where the image of newborn goats is likewise characterized by trembling.
v. 31-34 Et quoniam docui cunctarum exordia rerum / qualia sint et quam uariis distantia formis / sponte sua uolitent aeterno percita motu / quoue modo possint res ex his quaeque creari (III.31–34) is repeated verbatim at IV.45–48, serving as a formulaic didactic recapitulation of the atomic principles established in the first two books.
v. 31 Et quoniam docui (III.31) recurs identically at VI.43 and with slight structural variations at I.951, II.478, II.522 and IV.45, operating as one of Lucretius' standard transitions to link a completed argument to a new topic.
v. 32 qualia sint et quam uariis distantia formis (III.32) closely mirrors II.334 (qualia sint et quam longe distantia formis) to reintroduce the treatment of atomic shapes.
v. 33 uolitent aeterno percita motu (III.33) repeats verbatim the clausula at II.1055, deploying the same fixed formula to describe the incessant, unguided trajectory of atoms through the void.
v. 45 sed magis in primis animum (III.45) repeats the verse opening verbatim at III.181, deploying the same emphatic transitional formula to direct the reader's immediate attention to the nature of the mind (animus).
v. 62-63 noctes atque dies niti praestante labore / ad summas emergere opes (III.62–63) repeats verbatim the programmatic distich at II.12–13, deploying the same formulaic critique of blind human ambition and the exhausting struggle for political and material supremacy.
v. 87sq. nam ueluti pueri trepidant atque omnia caecis / in tenebris metuunt, sic nos in luce timemus (III.87–88): cf. ad II.55–56 (see also VI.35–36). The simile of the children frightened in the dark recurs verbatim from Book II; the indirect tradition (Seneca, Epist. 110, 6, reading ita for sic) is recorded there.
v. 87-93 nam ueluti pueri trepidant … sed naturae species ratioque (III.87–93) is repeated verbatim at II.55–61 and VI.35–41: a seven-line formulaic block sealing the proems of Books II, III and VI, stating that only reason, not wealth, power or sunlight, dispels human fears.
v. 91-93 hunc igitur terrorem animi tenebrasque necessest / non radii solis neque lucida tela diei / discutiant, sed naturae species ratioque (III.91–93) repeats verbatim I.146–148, where the tercet first states the programme of the poem: only the observation of nature and reason (naturae species ratioque) free the mind from terror.
v. 92 non radii solis neque lucida tela diei (III.92): cf. ad I.147 (see also II.60 and VI.40), where the image of the sun's rays as shining darts first appears and its parallels are recorded.
